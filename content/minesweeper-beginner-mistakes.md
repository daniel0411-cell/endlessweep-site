## 1. Guessing before scanning the whole board

One area may look blocked while another boundary contains a certain move. Inspect every open edge before accepting a guess.

## 2. Treating flags as facts

A flag records your decision; the game does not verify it. Build later deductions only on flags that are proven by visible numbers.

## 3. Counting only horizontal and vertical neighbors

Minesweeper numbers count all eight surrounding positions, including diagonals. Missing one diagonal square changes the entire deduction.

## 4. Reading one clue in isolation

Many positions require comparing overlapping neighbor sets. Subtract the shared candidates instead of repeatedly recounting one number.

## 5. Applying patterns by digits alone

A visible 1-2-1 is useful only when its covered-neighbor arrangement matches the full pattern. Extra covered squares can invalidate the shortcut. Review the [pattern conditions](/minesweeper-patterns/).

## 6. Chording with an unverified flag

A chord trusts the number of flags, not their locations. One misplaced flag can expose the real mine. Use the [chord checklist](/minesweeper-chord/) before clicking an open number.

## 7. Flagging every mine immediately

Flags are useful when they preserve information or enable a chord. Extra flag and unflag actions cost time and may distract from safe reveals.

## 8. Ignoring the remaining mine counter

The global count can resolve endgames and compare uncertain regions. If all remaining mines are constrained to one group, covered squares outside that group are safe.

## 9. Playing faster than you can verify

Speed comes from recognition, not skipping logic. First develop a consistent scan; then reduce hesitation and unnecessary actions.

## 10. Starting over without reviewing the loss

Use Mistake Review to return to the move before the mine. Ask whether the loss came from a wrong flag, a missed certain move, or a genuine guess. That distinction gives the next game a specific practice goal.
