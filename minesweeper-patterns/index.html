What are Minesweeper number patterns?

Minesweeper number patterns are recurring relationships between adjacent clues and their covered neighbors. Each number tells you how many mines touch that square. Comparing nearby clues as overlapping sets lets you prove which squares are safe and which contain mines instead of relying on luck.

Check the conditions first: Each diagram shows only part of a board. Apply a pattern only when no additional covered square changes a clue, existing flags have been counted, and the neighbor relationships match the example. Every pattern can also appear rotated or mirrored.

1-2-1 Pattern

■■■■■121··SAFE🚩SAFE■■

Green outline: safe to reveal. Flag: proven mine.

Logic: Assume the three clues share exactly three covered candidates and no other hidden square affects their counts. The outer 1s each require one mine, while the 2 requires two. Comparing those constraints forces the two outer candidates to be mines and the center candidate to be safe.

Safe move: Reveal the center candidate with a left click. Mark the two outer candidates as mines with a right click. The diagram rotates or mirrors without changing the deduction.

Practical tip: When you see 1-2-1 along a revealed edge, first verify that its covered strip contains exactly three candidates. Extra covered neighbors invalidate the shortcut.

1-2-2-1 Pattern

■■■■■1221·SAFE🚩🚩SAFE■

The two middle candidates are mines; the outer candidates are safe.

Logic: When the four clues correspond to exactly four covered candidates, comparing the left 1 with the adjacent 2 proves that the newly included middle candidate is a mine. The same comparison from the right proves the other middle mine. The two outer candidates are therefore safe.

Safe move: Reveal both outer candidates. Flag the two middle candidates, then use those flags to continue solving nearby clues.

Practical tip: Think of 1-2-2-1 as two edge comparisons meeting in the middle. Do not use it if any clue touches an additional unaccounted covered square.

2-1-2 Pattern

■■■■■212··🚩SAFE🚩■■

Compare the covered-neighbor sets before taking action.

Logic: This pattern is the complement of 1-2-1 in the standard three-candidate strip. The center 1 requires one mine among the candidates it shares, while each outer 2 needs one additional mine. Those additional outer candidates are mines, leaving the middle candidate safe. The conclusion depends on the exact covered-neighbor sets, not the digits alone.

Safe move: In the standard arrangement shown, reveal the middle candidate and flag both outer candidates. Pause if another covered square touches any of the clues.

Practical tip: Treat each clue's covered neighbors as a set and subtract the shared cells. This is safer than matching the visual sequence mechanically.

1-1-1 Pattern: A Boundary Case

■■■■■111··■■■■■

The sequence alone does not identify a specific mine.

Logic: Three consecutive 1s only say that each clue touches one mine. The clues may share the same mine, or different candidates may satisfy them. Without another constraint, the sequence usually has more than one valid solution.

Safe move: None of the covered squares can be safely revealed or flagged from 1-1-1 alone. Look for a nearby edge, flag, zero, or higher clue that removes one of the possible arrangements.

Practical tip: Stop when you see this sequence and map the neighbor set of each 1. Pattern recognition also means recognizing when a shape does not provide enough information.

Logic: A clue against a wall or corner has fewer possible neighbors. If the 2 sees exactly one candidate that the adjacent 1 does not see, that extra square must be a mine. After accounting for it, the remaining candidate beside the 1 is safe.

Safe move: Under the complete edge conditions shown, flag the extra candidate beside the 2 and reveal the green-outlined candidate beside the 1.

Practical tip: Count only neighbors that actually exist. Walls reduce the candidate sets and often make set subtraction easier than it is in the center of the board.
